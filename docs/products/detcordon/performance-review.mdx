This is a real, measured comparison — not a marketing estimate. We ran three
identical WordPress origins behind three different front doors and timed
every request. If you're deciding whether you need a hardened ingress
([gatekeeper](/products/articulate/gatekeeper)), a full malware-observation
layer ([DetCordon](/products/detcordon)), or neither, the numbers below are
the actual cost of each choice.

**In one sentence:** gatekeeper costs you almost nothing and stops a
narrow, real class of attacks; DetCordon costs you real request latency and
buys you full visibility into anything that gets through — they answer
different questions, and the right answer for most teams is "both, in
different places."

## The three configurations

| | What it is | What it does to a request |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Plain WordPress** | A stock WordPress install with nothing in front of it | Nothing — this is the baseline every other number is measured against |
| **[Gatekeeper](/products/articulate/gatekeeper)** | A thin Rust ingress: hardened upload endpoint, everything else passed straight through | Buffers the body, forwards it, and inspects file uploads specifically — no line-by-line inspection of ordinary traffic |
| **[DetCordon](/products/detcordon)** | A full observation tap: ModSecurity + the OWASP Core Rule Set, in both directions, plus eBPF telemetry | Runs the *entire* request and the *entire response* through the CRS rule engine before either one is allowed through |

Neither product is trying to be the other. Gatekeeper's job is to keep a
production WordPress site from getting popped in the first place, cheaply
enough to run on every request all the time. DetCordon's job is the
opposite: assume something *will* get through somewhere, and give you a
safe, contained place to watch it happen and capture what it drops. That
difference in purpose is exactly why the performance profiles below look so
different — DetCordon is not a worse gatekeeper, it is doing categorically
more work per request on purpose.

DetCordon's larger disk and memory footprint is almost entirely the OWASP
CRS ruleset and the ModSecurity engine holding it in memory — that's the
price of the same inspection depth that costs you the latency above. It is
not a leak or an inefficiency to fix; it's the ruleset.

### How this was measured

Three identical WordPress origins (SQLite-backed, no MySQL) served by PHP's
built-in server, each fronted by one of the three configurations above,
running on the same host. Latency was measured single-connection to isolate
per-request overhead from WordPress's own ceiling; throughput was measured
at 20 concurrent connections. CPU and memory were sampled from the relevant
proxy process during the concurrent run. This measures **added overhead**,
not absolute production throughput — a real deployment behind php-fpm and a
production-grade origin will have different absolute numbers, but the
*relative* cost of each front door should hold.

## DetCordon

**[DetCordon](/products/detcordon)** is a containment-first sandbox: it
assumes something will get through eventually, and gives you a safe,
observed place for that to happen instead of a silent compromise.

### Logos — the technical case

DetCordon runs the full OWASP Core Rule Set against both the request and
the response, in `DetectionOnly` mode, so nothing is ever blocked based on
a rule match that could be a false positive — every event is logged with
full context instead. Underneath that, eBPF telemetry watches syscalls
(`execve`, `connect`, `write`) at the kernel level, and Firecracker microVMs
give each detonation a hardware-isolated, disposable environment with a
hard TTL. Anything a compromised process drops — a webshell, a
dropper, a C2 payload — gets hashed, encrypted to your key, and shipped to
a separate sink host as evidence, automatically. The [performance numbers
above](#performance-metrics) are the honest cost of that depth: roughly
20-60ms of added latency at the percentiles that matter, in exchange for
inspecting a response body most WAFs never look at.

### Ethos — why trust it

DetCordon's own claims are checked, not asserted: the containment
model has a public [reviewer checklist](/products/detcordon/containment-assurance)
mapping every non-negotiable rule (sandbox/sink host separation, TTL
enforcement, `DetectionOnly` never silently upgrading to blocking, sample
bodies never printed in cleartext) to the exact code and test that
enforces it, so a technical buyer doesn't have to take our word for it. The
codebase carries a security-audit trail as a first-class practice, not an
afterthought bolted on before a sales call.

### Pathos — what's actually at stake

The realistic alternative to DetCordon isn't "nothing bad happens" — it's
finding out about a compromise from a customer, a breach-notification
lawyer, or a ransomware note, weeks after the fact, with no idea what got
in or what it took. DetCordon's entire value is turning that unknown into
a known: a hashed sample, a timeline of syscalls, a captured payload you
can hand to your incident response team the same day instead of the same
quarter. For a stakeholder signing off on the budget, that's the
difference between an incident that costs an afternoon of engineering time
and one that costs a disclosure filing.

**Best fit:** teams who run — or host on behalf of others — a WordPress (or
any web) property that's an attractive target, and want a real answer to
"what happens if we're wrong about our defenses" instead of hoping they
never find out.

## Gatekeeper

**[Gatekeeper](/products/articulate/gatekeeper)** is the opposite bet: keep
the obvious, common compromise path closed, as cheaply as possible, on
every single request, forever.

### Logos — the technical case

Gatekeeper's entire request path for ordinary traffic is a thin, bounded
pass-through — buffer, forward, done — with a dedicated hardened endpoint
for the one thing that actually matters for WordPress compromise in
practice: file uploads. `setup_wp.sh` checks every plugin and theme against
the Wordfence vulnerability feed *before* installation, the filesystem is
read-only except `wp-content/uploads` (enforced by `harden_runtime_fs.sh`),
dangerous PHP functions are disabled at the runtime level, and `auditd`
rules watch the immutable tree for anything that shouldn't change. The
[performance numbers above](#performance-metrics) show why this is
sustainable at scale: sub-millisecond overhead at every percentile, because
it isn't running a rule engine against every byte — it's closing a specific,
well-understood door.

### Ethos — why trust it

Gatekeeper doesn't just harden the runtime and hope — it ships its own
incident-response tooling (`incident_response.sh`, `auditd_incident_hook.sh`,
GPG-encrypted incident bundles) on the assumption that even a hardened
system deserves a real forensic path if something does go wrong. GraphQL
depth/complexity/introspection limits and client-cert-gated admin access are
built in, not optional add-ons. This is written by people who assume they
will eventually be tested, not people selling a guarantee.

### Pathos — what's actually at stake

Most WordPress compromises aren't exotic — they're an out-of-date plugin, an
uploads directory that accepts a `.php` file with a `.jpg` extension, an
admin panel reachable from anywhere on the internet. That's a boring,
preventable, entirely avoidable kind of bad day, and it's the one that ends
a lot of small businesses' relationship with their web presence overnight.
Gatekeeper's pitch to a customer or general audience is simple: the fix
for the common case shouldn't cost you anything you'd notice, and now it
doesn't.

**Best fit:** anyone running a production WordPress site who wants the
common compromise paths closed by default, with negligible performance
cost, and doesn't need (or can't afford the latency of) full
malware-capture depth on every request.

## Using both together

They're not mutually exclusive, and the performance numbers above are
exactly why layering them makes sense: gatekeeper in front of the public
site, cheap enough to run on every request forever; DetCordon fed a copy of
traffic — or standing in for a specific high-risk surface like an upload
endpoint or a staging environment — where the extra tens of milliseconds is
a cost you're willing to pay for the depth of what gets captured. Most
teams don't need to choose one architecture for the whole site; they need
the cheap door closed everywhere, and the expensive camera pointed at the
place they're most worried about.
